PART 0

Start here

If you read nothing else

The device is a motor that helps a weak wrist move, but only as much as needed. To make it behave safely we must know three things about the wrist (how heavy, how sticky, how springy it is), and we must trust a torque number we get from a twisting spring. Almost all of this document is about how we find those numbers, and how we know we can trust them.

0.1 What this device is

It is a wearable robot for the wrist. After a stroke, many people can move their wrist only a little. The device sits on the forearm and hand, and a small motor gives the wrist a helping push.

Two ideas shape everything:

  • Help only as much as needed. Recovery comes from the patient's own effort. If the robot does all the work, the patient stops trying. The robot must give less help when the patient does more.
  • Be safe. It is strapped to a person. Nothing it does may be sudden, too strong, or able to go out of control.

The wrist can move in two ways, and the device handles one at a time:

  • FE (flexion and extension): bending the hand toward the palm side, then back toward the back of the hand. Like waving "stop" and then "come here".
  • RUD (radial and ulnar deviation): swinging the hand sideways, toward the thumb and toward the little finger. Like a hand waving side to side in a "hello".

The project also has a second job: measuring the wrist. Doctors currently grade how stiff a wrist is with a 0 to 4 scale judged by feel (the Modified Ashworth Scale), and two doctors often disagree. The device can measure stiffness in real numbers. That measuring job turned out to be the more achievable and more defensible one (Part 1, step 7).

0.4 The whole system as one story

Read this once. Every later part zooms into one step of it.

1. Patient triesThe brain sends a signal. Muscles make tiny electrical signals (EMG).
→
2. Guess the effortSoftware reads the EMG and guesses how hard the patient is trying (the "intent").
→
3. Feel the pushA twisting spring between motor and wrist tells us how hard the wrist is being pushed.
→
4. Decide where to goA pretend spring-and-weight in software turns that push into a target position.
→
5. Move the motorThe servo motor goes to the target position.
→
6. Wrist respondsThe twist in the spring helps the wrist move. Angle sensors report the new angle.
↺

Steps 3 to 6 repeat 500 times every second. That repeating cycle is called the control loop. Steps 1 and 2 (the EMG guess) happen more slowly, 50 times a second, and feed into step 4 as a gentle hint.

In plain words

Think of a friend helping you push a heavy door. They feel how hard you are pushing, and push a little with you. If you stop pushing, they stop too. The spring is how they "feel" your push. The software is their decision about how much to help. The motor is their arm.

0.5 Words you need

Every technical word used later is explained here, in one place, with a picture from everyday life. Skim it now; come back whenever a word is unfamiliar.

Mechanics: the wrist as a physical object

Angle (θ)How far the wrist is bent, in degrees or radians. 360° is one full turn, which is about 6.28 radians. We use radians in equations and degrees when talking to people.
Torque (τ)A twisting force. Push 1 newton at the end of a 1 metre stick and you apply 1 newton-metre (N·m) of torque. A 50 g mass (about an egg) hanging on a 100 mm lever gives about 0.049 N·m.
Inertia (J)How hard it is to start or stop something turning. A heavy door is harder to swing than a light one. A hand has inertia too. Bigger J means slower to speed up and slower to stop.
Damping (B)Resistance that grows with speed. Stirring honey with a spoon: slow is easy, fast is hard. Damping is why a released hand does not swing like a pendulum forever. Think of the hydraulic arm at the top of a door that stops it slamming.
Stiffness (K)Resistance that grows with distance. A rubber band or spring: stretch it more and it pulls back harder. It is what pulls a bent wrist back toward its resting position.
Neutral angle (θ0)The angle where the springiness pulls neither way. Where the relaxed wrist would naturally rest. Not always zero degrees.
SpasticityAfter a stroke, muscles can overreact when stretched quickly. Move the limb slowly and little resistance; move it fast and the muscle "catches". It depends on speed, not just distance. This is the defining sign we want to measure.
Gravity torqueThe hand's own weight pulling it down. It confuses the measurements, so we remove it by testing with the motion sideways (horizontal), where gravity pulls straight down onto the supporting table instead of along the motion.
One picture that covers J, B and K

Imagine a door fitted with a spring hinge and a hydraulic closer. Push it open and let go. How heavy the door is is J. How strongly the spring pulls it shut is K. How much the hydraulic arm slows it down is B. A relaxed wrist behaves like exactly this door. The heavy-door, strong-spring, strong-closer combination decides whether it snaps back, swings past and wobbles, or creeps slowly home.

Measurement: how we get numbers from the real world

SensorAnything that turns a physical thing (angle, force, temperature, muscle signal) into a number the computer can read.
EncoderAn angle sensor. Ours splits one full turn into 4096 steps, so each step is 360 ÷ 4096 = 0.088°. It cannot report anything finer than that. We have two kinds: the motor's own encoder (θm) and a separate magnetic one on the wrist joint (θj, the AS5600).
QuantisationThe "staircase" effect of those steps. Like measuring with a ruler marked only in whole centimetres: you can only say 7 cm or 8 cm, never 7.4. The leftover error is called quantisation noise.
NoiseRandom jitter in a reading. A perfectly still joint can still show readings that flicker by a step or two, from tiny vibrations or electrical interference.
Hz and sampling rateHertz means "times per second". Recording at 500 Hz means the computer reads the sensors 500 times each second, once every 2 milliseconds. A 4 second recording at 500 Hz gives 2,000 readings, each with a time stamp.
AveragingTake many readings of something that is not moving, add them up and divide by how many. Random flicker evens out, so the average is steadier than any one reading. Careful: it only helps if the flicker really moves between steps. A reading stuck on one step stays stuck, however many you average.
EMGElectromyography: electrodes on the skin pick up the tiny electrical signal a muscle makes when it works. Its strength roughly follows how hard the muscle is working.

Control: how the machine decides what to do

ControllerThe software that decides what the motor should do next, based on what the sensors say.
Feedback loopMeasure, decide, act, measure again. A person steering a bicycle is a feedback loop. Ours runs 500 times per second.
Stable / unstableStable: after a bump, the system settles. Unstable: after a bump, it wobbles and the wobble grows. An unstable exoskeleton on a person is the main danger, so much of the design is about staying stable with room to spare.
Impedance and admittanceTwo ways to decide how a machine should feel when pushed. Impedance: "move me this far and I push back this hard". Admittance: "push me this hard and I move this far". Ours is admittance: it feels your push and answers with a movement.
Virtual modelA made-up spring, weight and damper that exists only in software. We choose its numbers (Md, Bd, Kd) to make the device feel the way we want. It is not the real wrist. The real wrist has Jh, Bh, Kh.
FeedforwardActing on a prediction instead of waiting for the result. The EMG guess is feedforward: it says "the patient is about to push" before the push shows up in the sensors.
ResonanceEvery springy connection between two weights tends to "ring" at one particular speed, like a plucked guitar string. If the control loop pushes at that speed, the ringing grows. We keep the loop well clear of it.

Testing: how we find the numbers and trust them

Transient and steady stateThe transient is the settling-down period right after a disturbance. Steady state is what remains once everything has settled. Test 1 studies the transient, because that is where J, B and K show themselves.
Underdamped and overdampedUnderdamped: it overshoots and wobbles a few times before stopping (a swing door with a weak closer). Overdamped: it creeps back without ever overshooting (a door with a very strong closer). A relaxed human wrist is often close to the line between the two, and may show no visible wobble at all.
Natural frequency (ωn) and damping ratio (ζ)Two numbers that describe how a transient looks. ωn is how fast it would swing if there were no damping. ζ says how heavily it is damped: below 1 it wobbles, above 1 it creeps. One recording gives us these two numbers, not J, B and K separately (see Test 1).
Curve fittingYou have dots (your measurements) and a formula with adjustable numbers. The computer adjusts the numbers, again and again, until the formula's curve passes as close to the dots as it can. The adjusted numbers are the answer.
R²A score from 0 to 1 for how well a curve fits the dots. 1 is perfect. 0.95 means the curve explains 95% of the ups and downs in the data. We use it as a quality gate: below the limit, we do not trust the fit.
HysteresisThe way there is not the way back. Bend a paper clip and release it: it does not return along the same path. In Test 0, loading weights up and then unloading them give slightly different readings. A big gap means something is slipping.
Repeatability (spread)Do the same test five times: do you get five similar answers? The spread is how far apart they are. Large spread means the method, or the way the test is done, is not consistent.
SurrogateA mechanical stand-in for a wrist, built from known parts, so we know its true inertia and stiffness in advance. We run every test on it first. If our method recovers the known answer on the rig, we can believe it on a person. If not, the fault is in our method, not in the person.
Pass criterionThe number a result must meet for the test to count as passed. Each test has one, and it is decided before the test, not after.
Negative controlA case where the answer should be nothing, run to prove the method does not invent effects. Example: a healthy person should show almost no spasticity.
Minimum detectable change (MDC)The smallest change in a measurement that is bigger than the device's own wobble between sessions. If the MDC is 2° and a patient improves by 1°, we cannot honestly say they improved.

PART 1

How we got here

The short version

Eight questions, each answer forced by the one before it. None of it is taste. If you can say the eight answers in order, you can defend the whole design, because when a reviewer attacks a decision the reply is always "because of the step above it".

01

What does the device actually have to do?

In plain words

Help a wrist that can move a little. Do not do the movement for the patient. Add just enough help that the patient finishes the movement, and no more.

Why not just move the wrist along a fixed path? That is called position control: the robot drags the hand through the planned motion no matter what the patient does. The patient can go completely limp and the motion still looks perfect. This is called slacking, and it is the best-known failure of rehabilitation robots. Recovery needs effort; a robot that removes the need for effort removes the recovery.

→ Plain position control is ruled out. The device must be easy to push around (backdrivable) and must change how hard it helps.
02

So we control how the device feels, not where it goes

In plain words

Instead of telling the robot a path, we tell it how it should behave when pushed: stiff like a spring, heavy like a flywheel, sticky like honey. Then the patient's own effort decides what happens.

There are only two ways to specify that, and they mirror each other:

  • Impedance control: measure the motion, command a force. "If you move me this far, I push back this hard."
  • Admittance control: measure the force, command a motion. "If you push me this hard, I move this far."

In any contact between two things, one side decides the force and the other decides the movement. Never both. That idea is called causality. Impedance is "force goes out". Admittance is "movement goes out". Same behaviour, opposite direction of the arrow.

Technical layer

Hogan (1985) formalised these as impedance (motion in, force out) and admittance (force in, motion out), the two causalities for physical interaction.

03

Which of the two can our motor actually do?

In plain words

Impedance control needs us to tell the motor "push with exactly this much force". Our motor does not accept that instruction. It only accepts "go to this position" or "spin at this speed".

Our servo (Feetech STS3215) takes commands over a serial bus in position mode or speed mode. It has no torque register. You cannot write a torque to it. Not "it is difficult": it is impossible.

→ Impedance control cannot be built on this motor. Admittance control it is. This comes from the datasheet, not from preference.

That one sentence fully answers the review question "why admittance and not impedance?" Remember it.

04

What does an admittance controller need?

In plain words

Something it can measure (how hard the wrist is being pushed) and something the motor accepts (a target position). We have both. In between sits a pretend spring-and-weight in software. Every 2 milliseconds the software works out how that pretend object would move under the current push, and gives its position to the motor as the target.

That is the entire controller. Part 2 is the mathematics of that one paragraph.

05

Where does "assist as needed" come from?

In plain words

The pretend spring has a stiffness, called Kd. Stiff pretend spring: it pulls the hand strongly toward the target, so the patient is helped a lot. Soft pretend spring: the hand is barely pulled, so the patient does the work. "Assist-as-needed" is just a rule for turning that one knob up and down.

We use five fixed settings, from very soft to quite stiff. It is not a different controller; it is a rule layered on top of the same one.

Why not simply fit a real spring? A real spring has one fixed stiffness and is always on. It cannot tell whether the patient is trying. Help that does not depend on effort is exactly what causes slacking.

06

Where does the machine-learning part fit?

In plain words

The ML model does not steer the machine. It only offers a hint: "the patient seems to be trying to bend the wrist, with about this much effort". That hint is added to the measured push as a small extra nudge. If the hint is late or wrong, the assistance feels a little sluggish or mistimed. It cannot make the machine unstable.

This is the most important structural fact in the project. The ML is outside the safety loop. That is why the link between the controller and the ML developer's work is a fixed agreement (a "contract") of five numbers sent 50 times a second. He builds to the contract; the controller never depends on his model's insides. The contract is written out in section 6.4.

→ Separate two things that were being treated as one. EMG is essential: nothing else tells us the patient is trying. Machine learning is not: the classical recipe (rectify, smooth, subtract the opposing muscle, threshold, scale) does the job in about forty lines.

So the classical method is built first as the baseline, and the ML becomes the challenger, compared against it. If ML wins, we have a result with a fair comparison. If it does not, we have an honest negative result, consistent with Wang 2026. Either way the assist mode works. (Decision D-21, proposed, pending team agreement.)

07

Why the main goal shifted to measurement

In plain words

At the supervisory review, a neurosurgeon said that stroke patients have no usable EMG in the hand. That is true for severe cases and not for the patients we target, but arguing it with no patient access and nine weeks left is a losing fight. So the lead goal became something that does not need patients or ML: measuring the wrist's mechanics objectively.

The device can measure inertia, damping, stiffness and the speed at which spasticity switches on, in real numbers. Today clinicians use an ordinal scale (Modified Ashworth) that depends on the examiner's feel and has poor agreement between examiners. The assist mode is now the second objective.

→ That claim needs no patients, does not depend on the ML working, can be proved wrong by data (so it is a real scientific claim), and can be delivered in nine weeks.
08

What the mechanism turned out to be

In plain words

Two directions of motion, one motor each. But the two are built differently. RUD (side to side) is pulled by a cable loop, and the cable stretches a little, like a stiff rubber band. FE (up and down) is driven directly, with a rigid link that does not stretch at all. That difference matters because the stretch is what we use to sense torque.

  • Torque can be applied both ways on both axes. There is no return spring and no direction that is left uncommanded.
  • RUD: the cable is the springy part. So the torque sensor is free, no load cell needed. FE: a rigid link has nothing to measure, so we add a deliberate series spring (section 2.3, D-23) to do the cable's job.
  • RUD has no backlash (no loose play), only slack, which a tensioner removes. FE's backlash is checked once its spring is fitted.
  • The two directions are mechanically coupled, so we use only one at a time. The two-channel EMG limit already forces that. One constraint, adopted for another reason, solves two separate problems.
History worth keeping

Decision D-14 first described both axes as cable loops. That was wrong from the start and D-23 corrects it.

2.1 The real wrist (the "plant")

In plain words

A relaxed wrist behaves like a door with a spring hinge and a hydraulic closer. It has weight (Jh), a spring that pulls it back to rest (Kh), and a "brake" that slows it in proportion to speed (Bh). After a stroke, a fourth effect appears: if the wrist is moved fast, the muscles push back hard (spasticity).

The equation just says: everything the wrist does by itself (left side) equals everything pushing on it (right side).

Why we do it

If we do not know how heavy, sticky and springy the wrist is, we cannot choose controller settings that stay stable, and we cannot make any claim about measuring it. Every number in this equation is something Part 4 goes and measures.

Jh θ̈ + Bh θ̇ + Kh(θ − θ0) + τspas(θ̇) = τdev + τgrav + τvol(2.1)

Read it left to right in words: weight × how fast the speed is changing, plus stickiness × speed, plus springiness × distance from rest, plus the spasticity reflex, equals the device's push plus gravity plus the patient's own muscle effort.

JhInertia of the hand plus the device's hand plate, about the wrist axis. kg·m². Weight-like.
BhDamping: soft tissue, joint fluid, tendon friction. Resists speed. N·m·s/rad. Honey-like.
KhPassive stiffness: the springy pull back toward rest. Resists distance. N·m/rad. Rubber-band-like.
θ0Neutral angle: where the springy pull is zero. Not necessarily 0°.
τspasThe spasticity reflex. Depends on speed. Defines the post-stroke wrist (Lance, 1980).
τdevThe torque the exoskeleton applies. This is our output. How it is produced is section 2.3.
τgravGravity pulling on the hand.
τvolThe patient's voluntary muscle effort. Unknown and uncontrolled. The EMG is trying to guess it.

Spasticity: the speed switch

Below a certain speed, nothing happens. Above it, the reflex resistance grows in proportion to how much faster you are going. Picture a car that has a speed limiter: no effect until the threshold, then it pushes back harder the further over you go.

τspas = bs · (|θ̇| − θ̇th)+ · sign(θ̇)(2.2)

Here θ̇th is the threshold speed, bs is how steeply the resistance climbs, the "+" means "ignore it if the bracket is negative" (that is the switch), and sign(θ̇) makes the reflex always oppose the direction of motion. Test 4 finds both bs and θ̇th.

Design decision that removes a whole unknown: keep gravity out

If the forearm is supported and the wrist moves in a horizontal plane, gravity pulls straight down onto the support and does not act along the motion. Then τgrav ≈ 0 and drops out of (2.1). One less number to identify, one less error source, and it costs only a forearm trough.

"Horizontal" depends on the axis. For RUD, rest the forearm palm-down on the table; the hand then swings sideways across the table. For FE, rest the forearm on its little-finger edge with the thumb up (a handshake position); the hand then bends sideways across the table. If you test FE with the palm down, the hand moves up and down and gravity is back in the equation. Write the posture into every test sheet.

What we expect

For a healthy relaxed wrist: Jh about 0.001 to 0.006 kg·m²; Bh about 0.005 to 0.08 N·m·s/rad; Kh about 0.43 to 1.89 N·m/rad (live-subject data, Falzarano). Spasticity bs close to zero on a healthy subject.

If it goes wrong

If you measure Kh near 8 to 23 N·m/rad, suspect the number, not the wrist. Those values come from cadaver wrists tested at the ends of the range of motion; they were wrongly applied to the near-neutral position once already (F-04). Check you are measuring close to neutral, with a relaxed subject.

Technical layer: the same wrist as a transfer function

Linearise about θ0 for small motions and take the Laplace transform with zero initial conditions. The result is the environment impedance: the torque needed per unit of resulting motion.

Zenv(s) = Jhs² + Bhs + Kh(2.3)

"Zero initial conditions" only means each test starts from rest at θ0. That describes how we run the experiment; it is not a hidden assumption.

2.2 The controller: a pretend wrist we design

In plain words

The controller is a small imaginary machine that lives only in software: an imaginary weight on an imaginary spring and damper. We feel the real push on the wrist, ask "how would my imaginary machine move under this push?", and tell the motor to go to wherever the imaginary machine would be.

This is the only part of the whole system we choose freely. By picking its numbers we decide how the device feels.

The most common mix-up

The real wrist (2.1) and the imaginary machine (2.4) are two separate equations. The imaginary machine is driven by τext (the push we sense). It does not equal τdev. The device torque τdev is created later, when the motor moves and the spring twists (section 2.3). The chain is:

τext → imaginary machine → θref → motor → spring twist → τdev → real wrist → new angle → back into τext

Why we do it

This is how we get "help only as much as needed" using a motor that only accepts positions (Part 1, steps 3 to 5). The patient's push moves the imaginary machine; the machine's position becomes the motor's target.

Md ẍ + Bd ẋ + Kd x = τext ,   x = θref − θ0(2.4)

In words: the imaginary weight times its acceleration, plus its damping times its speed, plus its stiffness times its displacement, equals the push. Every 2 ms we step this forward in time and hand the new position x to the motor.

How we picked the numbers

Choose the imaginary weight Md, how fast we want it to respond (natural frequency fn) and how much we want it to overshoot (damping ratio ζ). The stiffness and damping then follow:

Kd = Md(2πfn)² = 0.010 × (2π·2.0)² = 1.579 N·m/rad(2.6)
Bd = 2ζ√(KdMd) = 2(0.8)√(1.579 × 0.010) = 0.201 N·m·s/rad(2.7)
  • fn = 2 Hz because voluntary wrist rehabilitation movements are slower than about 1 Hz. The device should respond across the speeds the patient uses and ignore faster jitter.
  • ζ = 0.8 so it settles quickly without overshooting into the patient.
  • Md = 0.010 is picked using the stability rule below.

The stability rules that limit the choice

Md > Jh (necessary)   ·   Md ≥ 3Jh (adopted)   ·   Kd ≥ 0.6 Kh(2.8)
In plain words: why the imaginary weight must be heavier than the real hand

If the device is told to feel lighter than the real hand, it has to actively cancel part of the hand's real weight, by pushing harder than the push it feels. That needs high loop gain. High gain plus the small delay in the motor is the recipe for a wobble that grows. Feeling heavier than the hand costs nothing. With Jh ≈ 0.003 and Md = 0.010 the ratio is 3.3, inside the adopted rule.

What we expect

With these numbers the imaginary machine, given a steady push, settles in roughly 0.4 seconds (about 4 ÷ (ζ·2π·fn)), with almost no overshoot. The five assist levels are Kd = 0.4, 0.8, 1.6, 3.2, 6.4 N·m/rad; only Kd changes between levels.

If it goes wrong
  • Jerky or buzzing motion: usually delay or noise in the loop. Check the 8 to 10 Hz low-pass (section 2.6) and the loop timing (Test 7).
  • Device feels dead for a moment after hitting its limit: the anti-windup line is missing or wrong (section 6.3).
  • It pushes the patient when they are relaxed: the sensed torque has an offset (bad kθ, a slipped cable, or an unzeroed encoder). Re-run Test 0 and re-zero.
  • Check the softest assist levels against a stiff limb. The rule Kd ≥ 0.6 Kh with the stiffest expected Kh of 1.89 needs Kd ≥ 1.13. Levels 1 and 2 (0.4 and 0.8) fail that rule for stiff limbs. Confirm with the stability script before using them on anyone stiff.
Technical layer: admittance form

The admittance is the inverse of the target impedance:

Y(s) = 1 / Zd(s) = 1 / (Mds² + Bds + Kd)(2.5)

2.3 The transmission: a free torque sensor

In plain words

Between the motor and the wrist there is something slightly springy (the cable on RUD, a spring we add on FE). Think of a beam fixed at one end with a weight on the other: it bends, and the amount of bend tells you the weight. Here, the motor end is "fixed" and the wrist end is the free end. If the motor holds one angle and the wrist is pushed to another, the spring is twisted by the difference. Twist × stiffness = torque. It is the same idea as a bathroom scale, which measures weight by how much a spring squashes.

Both ends already have angle sensors: the motor's own encoder at one end, and the AS5600 at the wrist. So the torque sensor costs nothing extra.

Why we do it

A load cell (a dedicated torque sensor) is expensive, bulky and adds delay. The spring gives a torque reading at full loop speed with no new hardware. This is called a series elastic actuator (Pratt and Williamson, 1995). RUD has it by accident (the cable stretches); FE gets it by design (the added spring).

How we do it
  1. Read the motor angle θm and convert it to what the joint angle would be if the connection were rigid, using the pulley ratio. Call that θm,refl.
  2. Read the real joint angle θj.
  3. Subtract: Δ = θm,refl − θj. This is how much the spring is twisted.
  4. Multiply by the spring stiffness kθ to get torque.
θm,refl = θm · (rm / rj)   ·   Δ = θm,refl − θj   ·   τ̂j = kθ · Δ(2.9)

kθ is not a number to look up or guess. It is the output of Test 0, and everything downstream depends on it. RUD and FE are different parts, so each gets its own kθ (k_theta_rud and k_theta_fe in wrist_params.m).

The price: the staircase gets multiplied

In plain words

The torque comes from a difference of two angle readings, and each reading is a staircase with 0.088° steps. The steps' error is multiplied by the spring stiffness. A stiffer spring turns the same torque into a smaller twist, so the fixed-size steps look relatively bigger. That is why the spring must not be too stiff. It must also not be too soft, or it rings (section 2.6). There is a "just right" band.

The upgrade is still worth it for the identification tests, where the joint angle is read on its own: 227 steps across a 5° decay instead of 57. Use that reason.

2.4 The motor (actuator)

In plain words

When we tell the motor "go to this angle", it does not teleport. It takes a moment to start and then glides in, like a car pulling away. We describe that as a smooth lag, plus a small pure delay (the time for the command to arrive and the motor to react).

Gservo(s) = ωs² / (s² + 2ζsωss + ωs²) · e−sTd   fs ≈ 8 Hz, ζs ≈ 0.8, Td ≈ 5 ms(2.11)
Be honest about this

Those three numbers (8 Hz, 0.8, 5 ms) are assumed. Tests 7 and 8 replace them with measurements. Until then every stability margin quoted is provisional, and should be labelled that way.

2.5 What decides whether the loop is stable

In plain words

You have heard the squeal when a microphone gets too close to its own speaker: sound goes round, comes back louder, goes round again. A control loop can do the same. Send a small disturbance once around the loop and see what comes back. If it comes back bigger and arrives at the wrong moment so it adds to the original, the wobble grows. That is instability.

Loop gain is "how big it comes back". Gain margin and phase margin measure how much room we have before it tips over. More room is safer.

L(s) = [ Zenv(s) / Zd(s) ] · Gservo(s) · e−sTd(2.12)

Read the first bracket as a ratio: how resistant the real limb is divided by how resistant the pretend machine is. The stiffer the real limb compared with the pretend one, the larger the loop gain, and the nearer we are to the edge. That is why Kd has a lower bound set by Kh: you cannot render a soft device against a very stiff arm without the loop fighting itself. L(s) has no units because the units of the ratio cancel.

2.6 The ringing of the twisting spring (resonance)

In plain words

A spring with a weight at each end rings at one particular rate when it is plucked, like a tuning fork. Our transmission is exactly that: motor on one side, hand on the other. If the control loop pushes near that rate, the ringing builds up. We keep the loop well below it, and add a small filter as insurance.

RUD's cable and FE's spring are different parts with different stiffness, so there are two ringing rates, one per axis.

ωres = √( (kθ + Kh) / Jh )   (computed per axis)(2.13)

At kθ = 50 and light damping (ζ ≈ 0.05) the ringing peaks about +20 dB near 21 Hz. The loop's own speed is around 2 Hz and its response falls off steeply, leaving roughly 20 dB of margin at the peak. Acceptable, but compute it per axis rather than assume it, because it shrinks fast if kθ is lower than you think. The 30 N·m/rad floor in section 2.3 comes from here: require ωres to be at least twice the servo's ωs, and with Jh ≈ 0.003 that gives kθ of about 29.

Cheap insurance: three lines of code

Put a first-order low-pass filter at 8 to 10 Hz on the signal entering the controller. It barely affects the 2 Hz target and buys another 6 to 8 dB at the ringing peak. Also: wrist_stability.m currently assumes a rigid transmission, which is no longer true on either axis. The two-mass model is now the correct model, built once per axis. Do this before quoting any margin in the thesis.

PART 4

The tests: how we find the numbers

The short version

Fourteen tests, each answering one question about the hardware or the wrist. Every test has a purpose, a fixed procedure, a defined result and a pass limit. Every test runs on the mechanical surrogate before it runs on a person, so that a strange answer on a human can be blamed on the human or on our method, not both.

4.0 Five ideas used in every test

Read these once. The tests below simply use them.

4.0.1 What "recording at 500 Hz" means, and how it is done

In plain words

500 Hz means the computer reads the sensors 500 times a second, once every 2 milliseconds, and writes down each reading with the exact time it was taken. A 4 second recording gives 2,000 pairs of (time, angle). Those pairs are the "dots" we later fit curves to.

How it is done
  1. The PC script (decision D-22 moved the loop from the old ESP32 board to the PC) reads the joint encoder and the servo at a fixed pace.
  2. Every reading is stored with a time stamp t_us. The time stamp, never the sample number, is the truth about when something happened.
  3. The script checks for gaps. A gap longer than 3 ms in the 500 Hz log means the PC stalled, and that trial is suspect (section 5.4).

Open item: since D-22 the link between the AS5600 and the PC is not yet specified (it needs an I²C-to-USB bridge or equivalent). Until that is chosen, 500 Hz is a target, and Test 7 measures what is actually achievable.

4.0.2 Averaging, noise and the staircase

In plain words

A joint that is perfectly still still gives readings that flicker by a step or two (0.088° each). That flicker is noise. If the thing is not moving, averaging many readings gives a steadier answer than any single one. Averaging a position is not the same as calculating a speed: a speed needs the change in position divided by time. We average a still position only to reduce flicker.

The limit of averaging

Averaging only removes flicker that actually moves between steps. If a reading sits stuck on one step for the whole 5 seconds, the average is that step, and its rounding error stays. So a very small signal (only a step or two in total) is risky to measure, however long you average. Whenever a test relies on tiny angles, the test card says so and suggests a way to enlarge the signal.

4.0.3 Fitting a curve to the dots

In plain words

We have dots (the recording) and a formula that describes how a spring, weight and damper should behave. The formula has adjustable numbers. The computer does this over and over: (1) start with a guess; (2) draw the formula's curve; (3) measure how far the dots are from the curve; (4) nudge the numbers to make that gap smaller; (5) stop when it cannot get any smaller. The numbers it ends with are the answer. The method is called nonlinear least squares, and in MATLAB the search command is fminsearch. "Multi-start" means it is run from several different starting guesses, so it does not get stuck on a poor answer.

R² is the score for how close the curve ended up to the dots: 1 is perfect, and 0.95 means the curve explains 95% of the ups and downs in the data.

What one recording can and cannot tell you

A single released-hand recording determines two numbers: the natural frequency ωn and the damping ratio ζ. It cannot give J, B and K separately. If you scale all three by the same factor (double each), the curve is identical. To separate them you need extra information: a stiffness from Test 2, or a known added weight (Test 1b). The test cards say which.

4.0.4 Why every test runs on the surrogate first

In plain words

If we test a person and get Jh = 0.0041, we cannot tell whether that is their wrist or a bug in our method. So we first build a mechanical stand-in whose true inertia and stiffness we know (it is made from parts we can weigh and measure). We run the identical test and code on it. If the method returns the known answer, we have earned the right to trust it on a person. If it does not, we fix the method before any person is involved.

Technical layer

The surrogate's inertia is computed to ±0.26% (Test 1b). Surrogate files use SRG as the subject ID with the true values in the filename (for example SRG_J128K090_T1_FE_r01: J = 1.28×10⁻³, K = 0.90). The surrogate pass check is agreement with the known value within a few percent, not merely "inside the human range": a rig with known J has no excuse for a loose pass window.

4.0.5 Reading a pass criterion

  • Spread (repeatability): repeat a test five times. If the five answers differ by more than the stated percentage, the method or the way it is done is inconsistent. Spread is measured as the range of the answers divided by their mean.
  • Hysteresis: load up, then unload, and compare. Defined here as the largest gap between the up curve and the down curve at the same load, divided by the full-scale reading. A big gap means something is slipping, sticking or creeping.
  • Pass limits are fixed before the test. Changing a limit after seeing the result is how people fool themselves.

TEST 0

Transmission stiffness

yields kθ: gates the torque estimate and therefore everything downstream

In plain words

We hang known weights on a lever attached to the hand plate, while the motor holds still. Each weight twists the transmission spring a little. More weight, more twist. If we plot weight-torque against twist, the slope of the line is the spring's stiffness, kθ. It is the same trick as calibrating a bathroom scale: put on known weights, see how far the spring moves.

Picture a beam fixed at one end with weights added to the free end: the more weight, the more it bends. Here the motor end is the "fixed" end. It is never perfectly fixed (the motor holds its position through a control loop, and the gears have some play), so we record both encoders, the motor's and the joint's, and use the difference.

Why this test, and why first

Every torque number in this document (the identification tests, the stability margins, the assist controller itself) is computed from Eq. 2.9, and Eq. 2.9 is only as good as the single constant kθ that multiplies the twist. Measuring kθ directly, instead of trusting a datasheet or a hand calculation, turns "the cable or spring is a torque sensor" from a claim into a calibrated instrument.

Setup

Run it once per axis. RUD and FE are different transmissions (D-23), and each needs its own kθ.

  • RUD: device assembled, cable pre-tensioned to a recorded setting, both encoders live. Joint free. A lever of measured length clamped to the hand plate.
  • FE: the same rig, but there is no cable to tension; the only springiness is the added series spring. If you want the "rigid link" number for the thesis, also run it on the bare direct link before the spring is fitted. It should land near the 1000 row of the table in section 2.3 (twist almost zero, about 64% noise). That is the evidence that motivated the spring, not a failed test.

Steps

  1. Command the servo to hold a mid-range position. Leave it there for the whole test.
  2. Hang a known mass on the lever. Torque is mass × gravity × lever length: τ = m·g·L. Start with 50 g on 100 mm: 0.05 × 9.81 × 0.1 = 0.0491 N·m.
  3. Record both encoder readings for 5 seconds, then average each (the weight is not moving, so averaging just steadies the reading).
  4. Repeat for 50, 100, 150 and 200 g. That is one up sweep. Then take the weights off in reverse order, 200, 150, 100, 50 g, as the down sweep. Comparing the two sweeps gives the hysteresis.
  5. Plot torque τ against twist Δ = θm,refl − θj. The slope is kθ.

The points lie close to a straight line through the origin, and the up and down sweeps nearly overlap. The slope is between 30 and 250 N·m/rad. RUD usually lands at 50 to 200; FE's spring was sized for 40 to 60.

If it goes wrong
  • Slope above 250 (too stiff): the twist is so small the staircase noise dominates. Add compliance: on RUD, add a series spring; on FE, size the spring softer (a stiffer spring would push kθ higher).
  • Slope below 30 (too soft): the transmission will ring too close to the control speed. On RUD, increase pre-tension and repeat. On FE there is no tension knob: the fitted spring itself is too soft and must be re-sized stiffer.
  • Points curve instead of lying on a line: the cable or spring is not behaving elastically. Look for a slipping clamp, a cable running over an edge, or a spring bottoming out.
  • The up and down sweeps are far apart: something slips or creeps. Dyneema cable stretches slowly; re-tension and wait before repeating.

TEST 1

Free oscillation

yields ωn and ζ, from which Bh, and Jh once Kh is known · script wrist_id_freeosc.m

In plain words

The test is nothing more than this: the examiner lifts the relaxed hand a few degrees away from rest and lets go, cleanly, without pushing. The hand goes back to rest by itself, and we record how it goes back. Think of releasing a door fitted with a spring hinge and a closer. A quick snap-back with a small wobble tells you one thing about the door; a slow creep home tells you another.

What pulls the hand back is the springiness of the wrist's tissue (Kh), not gravity (the hand moves sideways, where gravity does not act along the motion). What slows it is the stickiness (Bh). What makes it slow to get going and slow to stop is its weight (Jh). All three show up in the shape of the return.

Why this test

It is the simplest way to see the wrist's own mechanics with the least equipment in the way. There is no torque sensor and no servo command in the measurement: only the joint encoder. The fewer things between the wrist and the number, the fewer ways the number can be wrong.

"Servo off" is not the same as "nothing attached"

A servo with its torque disabled still has gears that rub and a heavy motor rotor. Through the gear ratio, that rotor can feel many times heavier to the wrist than it really is. And if the plate is still joined to the motor through the twisting spring, the arrangement is no longer one swinging mass; it is two masses joined by a spring, and a single-mass curve will fit it badly. The result would be "limb + plate + part of the motor", not the wrist.

Therefore: (1) uncouple the transmission from the hand plate for this test (cable off, or the servo mechanically disconnected) so that only the limb and the plate swing. If the design truly cannot be uncoupled, write that down as a limitation and report Jh as "limb + plate + drivetrain". (2) Run a plate-only baseline with no limb, first on the surrogate, to measure the rig by itself. This is what lets "Jh = limb + plate" be a statement you have checked, not assumed.

Posture (gravity must stay out)

  • RUD: forearm resting palm-down on a supported trough or table. The hand swings sideways across it.
  • FE: forearm resting on its little-finger edge, thumb up (handshake position). The hand bends sideways across the table.

Steps

  1. Subject fully relaxed, forearm supported, correct posture for the axis, transmission uncoupled, servo off.
  2. The examiner moves the hand 5 to 10° from neutral and releases cleanly: no push, no follow-through.
  3. Record the joint angle at 500 Hz for 4 seconds (2,000 time-stamped readings; section 4.0.1). Record the EMG at the same time.
  4. Repeat 5 times for each direction (released from the flexion side, and from the extension side; or from the radial and ulnar side for RUD). Discard any trial where the EMG shows voluntary muscle activity. A subject who helped or resisted is not a relaxed wrist.

What the computer does with the recording

It fits the whole curve (all 2,000 dots) with a formula for a weight on a spring and damper, using multi-start fminsearch (section 4.0.3). The fit returns two numbers: the natural frequency ωn and the damping ratio ζ. It does not return J, B and K separately; one recording cannot (section 4.0.3). The remaining steps are:

  • Kh from Test 2 (slow stretch), then Jh = Kh / ωn².
  • Or Jh and Kh together from Test 1b (added weight).
  • Then Bh = 2·ζ·Jh·ωn.
Failure F-01: why we do not use the log-decrement method

The classic textbook method reads two successive wobble peaks and computes the damping from their ratio. It failed validation badly: at a realistic ζ = 0.375 there are only one or two usable peaks, and the wrist may be overdamped, with none at all. The recovered ωn came out four orders of magnitude wrong. Full-curve fitting uses every sample instead of two peaks. This is in the thesis as a documented method rejection.

What we expect

With typical numbers (Kh about 1 N·m/rad, Jh about 0.003 kg·m², ζ about 0.375) the hand returns to rest within roughly 0.6 seconds, with one or two small overshoots, then sits still for the rest of the 4 seconds. The fit gets R² of 0.95 or better. The five repeats agree within 20%.

The wrist may also be overdamped and creep home with no visible wobble. That is not a failed test; the method handles both cases.

If it goes wrong
  • R² low on one trial: the subject was probably not relaxed. Look at the EMG from that trial before blaming the fit.
  • Each fit is good but the five answers differ by more than 20%: the examiner's release is inconsistent (a push, or a slow, soft let-go). Re-brief and repeat. Do not average the problem away.
  • The curve starts with a bump or a kick: a push at release. Same fix.
  • No wobble at all: the wrist is overdamped, and the curve is close to a single exponential. ωn is then poorly determined and so is Jh. Report the uncertainty instead of a falsely exact number.
  • Jh outside its window on the surrogate rig, whose true J is known: the fault is in the method or code, not the rig. Do not collect human data until it is resolved.
Pass: fit R² ≥ 0.95; spread across the 5 trials under 20%; Jh within 0.001 to 0.006 kg·m² on a human; on the surrogate, Jh within a few percent of the rig's known value (section 4.0.4).
Technical layer

Free response of Jhθ̈ + Bhθ̇ + Kh(θ − θ0) = 0 from initial displacement and zero velocity. For ζ < 1: θ(t) = θ0 + A·e−ζωnt·cos(ωdt + φ), with ωd = ωn√(1 − ζ²), ωn = √(Kh/Jh), ζ = Bh / (2√(JhKh)). For ζ > 1 the response is a sum of two decaying exponentials; the fit covers both. Because the free response depends on J, B and K only through ωn and ζ, J, B and K are not individually identifiable from this test alone.

TEST 1b

Added inertia

yields Jh and Kh from the encoder alone: no torque sensor needed

In plain words

Test 1 gives one clue about the wrist: how fast it swings (ωn). But a heavy wrist with a strong spring can swing at the same speed as a light wrist with a weak spring, so one clue cannot tell weight from springiness. The fix: repeat Test 1 after bolting a known extra weight to the hand plate. The wrist now swings more slowly, and how much slower depends on how heavy the wrist already was. Two speeds, one known weight, and the maths can separate the two unknowns.

Why this test

Test 1 gives ωn. Jh and Kh are two unknowns, so ωn alone cannot separate them without assuming one. Adding a precisely known inertia ΔJ and repeating the same test gives a second, independent equation at a second, known frequency, which is enough to solve for both without assuming either. No torque sensor is needed.

Steps

  1. Run Test 1 bare. Fit ω1.
  2. Bolt the known inertia ΔJ to the hand plate. Run Test 1 again in exactly the same way (same posture, same release, same transmission uncoupling). Fit ω2.
  3. Solve for J and K.
J = ΔJ·ω2² / (ω1² − ω2²)   ·   K = J·ω1²(4.1)

Where it comes from: the stiffness is the same in both runs, so K = J·ω1² = (J + ΔJ)·ω2². Rearranging gives J.

Building ΔJ

Two 100 g masses at r = 80 mm on a light cross-arm give ΔJ = 2mr² = 1.28×10⁻³ kg·m². That is a 43% change on a typical Jh of 0.003, well above the noise. With mass known to ±0.01 g and radius to ±0.1 mm, ΔJ is uncertain by ±0.26% (0.01% from the mass plus twice 0.125% from the radius). That number goes straight into the thesis as the traceability chain. Weigh the cross-arm and include its own small inertia, or measure it in the plate-only run; otherwise it is an unaccounted part of ΔJ.

What we expect

The loaded swing is slower. With Jh = 0.003 and ΔJ = 0.00128, ω2 should be about 84% of ω1 (√(0.003/0.00428)). J and K should agree with the J from Tests 1 and 2 and the K from Test 2 within 15%.

If it goes wrong
  • J or K disagrees with the other tests by more than 15%: that is information, so chase it; do not average it away. Likely causes, in order: the masses were not at the stated radius; the cross-arm's inertia was ignored; the strap or mount shifted between the two runs; the subject was less relaxed in one run.
  • ω2 is not clearly lower than ω1: the added inertia did not actually couple to the swinging part, or the fit is poor. Check both R² values first.
Pass: J and K agree with Tests 1 and 2 within 15%.
TEST 2

Quasi-static ramp

yields Kh, θ0 · script wrist_id_static.m

In plain words

Move the wrist very slowly through its range while the torque is recorded. Slowly enough that speed-dependent resistance (damping, the spasticity reflex) is negligible. What is left is pure springiness: the further you stretch, the more it pulls back. The slope of "pull-back torque against angle" is Kh.

Why this test

It isolates stiffness from the other effects, by taking speed out of the picture. At 5°/s (0.087 rad/s), even the largest expected damping (0.08 N·m·s/rad) adds only about 0.007 N·m, while the spring torque at 30° is about 0.5 N·m, so damping is around 1% of the signal.

Steps

  1. Drive the joint very slowly (under 5°/s) through ±30°, so inertial and damping effects are negligible and only stiffness remains.
  2. Record the torque estimate τ̂ and the angle θ throughout. Three sweeps in each direction.
  3. Fit K separately on each side of neutral over a stated angular window. The wrist is not equally stiff both ways, so one number for both sides would hide something real.
What we expect

A gently curving torque-angle line, steeper further from neutral. Kh between 0.2 and 3.0 N·m/rad, usually 0.43 to 1.89. The script prints the comparison with the Falzarano literature range automatically. A thin loop between the forward and return paths (hysteresis) is normal; its area is reported, not hidden.

If it goes wrong
  • Kh far above 3: the subject is not relaxed (muscles pushing back), or the forearm shell is flexing (Test 6), or the window includes the very end of the range.
  • A wide loop: the tissue is creeping or the sweep is faster than 5°/s.
  • Different slopes on the two sides: expected, not a fault.
Pass: Kh in 0.2 to 3.0 N·m/rad; hysteresis loop area reported, not hidden; the script prints the Falzarano literature check (0.43 to 1.89) automatically.
TEST 3

PRBS perturbation

yields Zenv(jω) across the band · scripts wrist_prbs.m, wrist_id_perturb.m

In plain words

Instead of one slow stretch or one release, we gently jiggle the wrist back and forth with a pattern that looks random but is exactly known (a PRBS, pseudo-random binary sequence). That pattern contains many speeds at once. We then see how strongly the wrist resists at each speed. It is like tapping a wine glass with many different notes at once to see which ring.

Why this test

It is the only test that gives J, B and K at the same time and independently, as a response across a range of speeds rather than three separate fits. It is the one a reviewer will respect most, because agreement between this and Tests 1 and 2 is strong evidence that the model is right.

Steps

  1. Generate the sequence: wrist_prbs(10, 0.025, 1000, deg2rad(4)). Order 10 (a pattern of 1,023 steps), 25 ms per step, ±4° amplitude. The pattern lasts about 26 seconds.
  2. Command it as a small position jiggle about neutral. The subject is relaxed and not reacting.
  3. Record the angle θ and torque τ̂ at 500 Hz for 30 seconds.

What the computer does

It computes how strongly the torque responds at each frequency (cpsd and pwelch), and a coherence score (mscohere). Coherence, from 0 to 1, says how much of the response at a given frequency is truly caused by the jiggle. Every frequency where coherence is below 0.8 is thrown away, since it is noise dressed up as data. Then it fits J, B and K to what is left by complex least squares.

What we expect

Coherence at or above 0.8 across at least 1 to 10 Hz. Fitted J, B and K within 25% of the values from Tests 1 and 2.

If it goes wrong
  • Low coherence: the subject is reacting to the jiggle (voluntary response), or the motion is too small compared with the noise. Re-brief to relax and not resist.
  • Fitted values disagree with Tests 1 and 2 by more than 25%: treat it as a finding, and look for the cause (posture, mount, transmission still coupled) before choosing which test to believe.
Pass: coherence ≥ 0.8 across at least 1 to 10 Hz; fitted J, B, K agree with Tests 1 and 2 within 25%.
Technical layer

Estimate Z(jω) = τ̂(jω)/θ(jω) (torque per unit angle, matching Eq. 2.3) from cross and auto spectra; fit [real(A); imag(A)] \ [real(Z); imag(Z)] to Zenv(jω) = −Jhω² + jBhω + Kh over the unmasked bins.

TEST 4

Velocity-graded ramps

yields bs, θ̇th · script wrist_id_spasticity.m

In plain words

Spasticity is a reflex that depends on speed: move the wrist slowly and the muscle stays quiet; move it fast enough and the muscle "catches" and pushes back. So to find it, we move the wrist through the same range at several different fixed speeds and see at which speed the extra resistance appears and how fast it grows. This test produces the headline clinical result.

Steps

  1. Drive constant-speed ramps through the same angular range at 10, 30, 60, 120 and 240°/s.
  2. Record the torque estimate and angle throughout each ramp. Two passes per speed, in random order, with at least 60 seconds of rest between passes. A patient protocol is two passes total.
  3. Subtract the ordinary, non-reflex resistance. From Tests 1 to 3 we know how a wrist with no reflex would resist (its J, B and K). Whatever resistance is left over is the reflex.
  4. Fit the leftover: search for the threshold speed (grid search), and fit the slope above it (least squares).
Failure F-06: why this is two passes and not fifteen

The original protocol was 5 speeds × 3 repeats, swept slowest to fastest. A physiotherapist in Interview 2 said: "we flex and extend at most twice; we do not exceed that." That describes thixotropic softening: repeated stretching of the same limb progressively lowers its resistance, like a rubber band that loosens after being stretched. Sweeping from slow to fast would have confused "repetition number" with "speed" systematically, so the softening would have looked exactly like a speed effect. The fix (random order, few passes, long rests) was caught before any subject was tested.

What we expect

The leftover resistance rises steadily with speed. The threshold lands between 0.2 and 3 rad/s (about 11 to 170°/s, which is why the test speeds run from 10 to 240°/s). On a healthy subject, bs should come out close to zero. That is the negative control, and you must show it.

If it goes wrong
  • Healthy subject shows clear spasticity: suspect the passive model (re-check Tests 1 to 3) or a tense subject, not a discovery.
  • Leftover is flat on a spastic subject: the threshold may be above 240°/s, or the passive model is over-subtracting.
  • Leftover goes up and down with no pattern: check the random order was really used, and the rest time kept.
Pass: residual torque rises monotonically with speed; threshold lands between 0.2 and 3 rad/s. On a healthy subject bs is near zero.
TEST 2b

End-feel index

yields Kend, Kend/Kmid · script wrist_id_endfeel.m · no new experiment: runs on Test 2 data

In plain words

Clinicians talk about "end-feel": how the resistance builds as the joint nears the end of its range. A hard end-feel is like running into a wall (resistance shoots up); a soft one builds gradually. Doctors judge it by hand. Test 2 already recorded the torque against angle, so the same curve can give a number for it for free.

In Interview 2, a physiotherapist explained the Ashworth grade this way: "It relies heavily on tactile feedback ('end-feel')... what feels stiff to me might feel less stiff to a male physio with more physical strength." That is the mechanism of the poor agreement between examiners, in a practitioner's own words: the grade depends on the examiner's own strength.

  1. Take the Test 2 torque-angle curve.
  2. Fit the slope over the middle band, and separately over the last 20% of the range.
  3. The ratio of the two slopes is the index.
What we expect

An index between about 1 (the curve is straight) and 4 or more (a sharp wall at the end).

Report: alongside the Ashworth grade, never instead of it. The comparison between the two is the point.
TEST 4b

Stretch-conditioning decay

yields the softening time constant: the confound turned into a measurement

In plain words

Test 4 revealed that stretching the same limb again and again makes it progressively easier to stretch. Instead of treating that as a nuisance, this test measures it: stretch a healthy limb ten times in a row at one fixed speed, and see how the resistance falls.

Healthy subjects and the surrogate can take unlimited passes. Use a fixed speed and 10 consecutive passes. Measure the peak resisting torque on each pass, then fit the decay. Report the time constant, the loss between pass 1 and pass 10, and the recovery after a stated rest.

Three returns for ten minutes of work: it quantifies an effect no clinical scale captures; it justifies the short patient protocol from our own data instead of by convention; and it supplies the rest time between passes, which the protocol currently guesses.

If it goes wrong

If the peak torque does not fall steadily, check the speed was identical every pass and the subject stayed relaxed. A non-monotonic decay on the surrogate (which cannot soften) points at the measurement chain.

Pass: monotonic decay, fitted time constant with R² ≥ 0.9, and a stated recovery interval.
TEST 5

Inter-DOF coupling

yields c12: decides whether treating the two axes as independent (decision D-06) survives

In plain words

Two motors on one hand plate can fight each other. If moving one axis forces the other motor to work to hold still, the axes are not independent and the single-axis maths is wrong. This test measures how much.

Hold axis 2 at zero. Sweep axis 1 slowly through ±45°. Record the torque axis 2 has to produce to stay put. The ratio of that sideways torque to the main torque is c12. The torque of axis 2 is read from its own torque-sensor estimate (Eq. 2.9) when that axis has one, or from the servo's load register as a stand-in. FE's spring not being sized yet does not block this test (PHYSICAL_BOM §7.5, D-23 consequence 4).

If it goes wrong

c12 above 0.10 means one of two things: model the coupling properly (a Jacobian), or constrain the protocol so only one axis ever moves at a time. The mode-switched operation already does the second, which is the argument you make either way.

Pass: c12 < 0.10, so independent axes are defensible in writing with the number quoted.
TEST 6

Shell rigidity

yields Ks: a go / no-go on the mechanical design

In plain words

The forearm shell is the "ground" that every angle is measured against. If the shell flexes, the angle we read is not the real joint angle, and every number in the thesis is wrong. Note that this is the opposite of the transmission spring: springiness in the drive path helps (it is our torque sensor); springiness in the reaction path destroys our reference frame.

Why the limit is 36 N·m/rad

The shell is in series with the limb, so we measure a blend: Kmeas = KsKh / (Ks + Kh). For less than 5% error on the stiffest limb we expect (Kh = 1.89), the shell must be at least 19 times stiffer than the limb:

Ks ≥ 19·Kh,max = 19 × 1.89 = 36 N·m/rad  →   deflection < 1.6° at 1 N·m(4.2)

Steps (about ten minutes)

  1. Clamp the shell the way it will be worn.
  2. Hang a mass on a measured lever to apply 1.0 N·m (for example 1 kg on a 102 mm lever).
  3. Read the angular deflection with a dial gauge at a known radius.
If it fails

The shell must be stiffened before anything else is built on it: thicker walls, ribs, a stiffer material, or a better clamp. A failure found in November sinks everything, so do this test this week.

Pass: under 1.6° at 1 N·m. Target under 0.5°.
TEST 7

Loop timing

yields Td and the actual achievable loop rate

In plain words

The design assumes the control loop completes every 2 milliseconds. A PC running a normal operating system does not promise that; it can pause for a moment to do something else. This test finds out what really happens: how long each loop takes, how often it is late, and how long a full conversation with the motors takes.

Budget at 500 Hz is 2 ms per tick. Estimated bus time at 1 Mbps is 0.6 to 1.4 ms, which is feasible but tight, and it is only an estimate.

  1. Log a microsecond time stamp at the start of every loop tick for 60 seconds, and plot a histogram of the gaps between ticks.
  2. Separately time one full bus transaction: write both position commands in one packet, then read both positions back.
Wording updated for D-22

Rev D described toggling a GPIO pin inside an interrupt routine and watching it on a scope. That was for the old embedded board. Since D-22 the loop is a PC script, so logged time stamps are the main method. A scope check needs a pin the PC can toggle, which depends on the (still unspecified) USB bridge.

If it goes wrong

If ticks overrun 2 ms, read the servos at 250 Hz and keep the joint-encoder loop at 500 Hz. The multi-rate design in Part 6 exists for exactly this.

Pass: worst-case tick under 2 ms with no overruns over 60 seconds.
TEST 8

Servo step response

yields fs, ζs: replaces the assumed values in Eq. 2.11

In plain words

Tell the motor "jump 5° now" and watch how it gets there: how fast, and whether it overshoots. That tells us the motor's own speed and smoothness, which section 2.4 had to assume.

  1. Lock the joint to a known inertia (the surrogate, with no spring).
  2. Command a 5° position step. Record the joint encoder at 500 Hz.
  3. Fit a second-order response (the same kind of curve fit as Test 1).
  4. Repeat at three step sizes (to check it behaves the same for small and large steps) and with two different loads.
If it goes wrong

Outside the limits below, the motor model used in the stability analysis is wrong, and every margin must be recomputed. Different results at different step sizes mean the motor is not behaving like a simple lag.

Pass: fs in 5 to 15 Hz, ζs in 0.5 to 1.2.
TEST 9

Mount / remount repeatability

yields the minimum detectable change (MDC): the single most publishable number in the project

In plain words

The encoder can read 0.088°, but that is not what a clinician cares about. They care about this: take the device off, put it back on, and measure again. How much does the answer move just from re-fitting? Strap tension, where the limb sits, soft tissue settling and finger position all matter, and none of them appear on a datasheet. The size of that movement is the smallest improvement we can honestly call real.

  1. Run Tests 1 and 2 on the subject.
  2. Remove the device completely. Put it back on, including re-setting the neutral offset and the finger constraint.
  3. Repeat, for three mounts, in the same session by the same operator.
  4. Report the standard deviation of Jh, Bh and Kh across mounts, and the 95% MDC.
Where the pass limit comes from

In Interview 2, a physiotherapist said early-phase goals progress about 1 cm of hand lift per week: "if our target in the first week is about 2 centimetres, we increase it to 3 the following week, then 4." At 8.5 cm (the knuckle) to 18 cm (the fingertip) from the wrist axis, 1 cm is about 3 to 7° of wrist angle per week. Taking one third of that gives the requirement:

MDC ≤ 2°, ideally ≤ 1°. That is a specification traced from what a physiotherapist actually does, not a number chosen to flatter the hardware.

If it goes wrong

An MDC above 2° means re-seating the device moves the answer by more than a typical weekly improvement. Look at the strap, the neutral-offset step, and the finger constraint before blaming the electronics. Report the real number anyway: a large honest MDC is still a result.

Why this is the paper: without an MDC figure, "the device measures wrist impedance" cannot be proven wrong. With it you can write "the minimum detectable change in Kh is X N·m/rad, against an Ashworth scale granularity of one ordinal grade."
Technical layer

A common definition is MDC95 = 1.96 × √2 × SEM, where SEM is the standard error of measurement (the within-subject standard deviation across mounts). State the formula you use.

TEST 11

Goniometer concurrent validity

yields Bland-Altman agreement with clinical practice

In plain words

A goniometer is the clinician's protractor. It measures the hand relative to the forearm. Our encoder measures the device's own joint angle. These are not automatically the same number: misaligned axes, a slipping strap or moving soft tissue can make them differ. If our degrees are not the clinic's degrees, none of our range-of-motion output means anything clinically, and the Test 9 comparison collapses.

  1. For each subject, measure the range of motion at the same time with the device and with a manual goniometer, across the range, with several repeats, on both axes.
  2. Plot the difference between the two readings against their average (a Bland-Altman plot). Report the bias (the average difference) and the 95% limits of agreement (bias ± 1.96 standard deviations).

Cost: one goniometer (under USD 10) and twenty minutes per subject. This test earns the sentence "the device's angle output agrees with clinical goniometry to within ±X°". Without it, no claim of clinical interpretability can be made at all.

Open: no numeric limit is set

Rev D gives no number for "agrees well enough". Decide the acceptable limits of agreement before collecting data, and tie them to the Test 9 requirement (MDC ≤ 2°). open

PART 6

The loop, concretely

The short version

Each tick (every 2 ms) the loop reads two angles, turns their difference into a torque, adds the ML hint, runs the pretend model one step, applies safety limits, and writes one target angle to the motors. Then it does it again.

6.1 Inputs and outputs

Inputs

  • θj: joint angle, AS5600, 500 Hz, I²C. The link to the PC is open since D-22 dropped the ESP32 (it needs an I²C-to-USB bridge or equivalent, not yet specified).
  • θm: motor angle, servo bus, 200 Hz.
  • τ̂intent: from ML, 50 Hz, held between updates, five fields (section 6.4).
  • E-stop: hardware button, checked every tick.

Outputs

  • θref: the target angle written to the STS3215, 500 Hz, to both servos in one packet.
  • The log: every channel in section 5.1, written directly by the PC script.
  • The sync pulse: GPIO to the MP36. Lost with the ESP32 (D-22); not yet replaced (section 5.1).
In plain words: how can a position-controlled motor give a "feel"?

The loop never asks for a torque. It asks for a position. The motor goes there; the spring between motor and wrist twists; the twist is the torque, and the torque moves the wrist. On RUD the motor pulls the cable, and the cable winding up is the torque. On FE the motor moves a rigid link, and the added spring winding up is the torque. So the loop commands the position that produces the torque. That is what admittance control is, and it is the sentence to say when someone asks how a position-controlled servo can create a feel.

6.2 Three rates, one design

In plain words

Not everything needs to update equally fast. The motion loop must be fast (500 times a second) because delays there can cause shaking. The torque reading can be slower (200 times a second) because the springiness we care about is 10 times slower still. The ML hint can be slowest (50 times a second) because muscle effort changes slowly. Between its updates, the loop simply holds the last hint (a "zero-order hold"), like a clock hand that jumps once every 20 ms and stays put in between.

6.3 The controller, in code

This runs as a PC script now, not embedded firmware (D-22). The logic is unchanged by the move; only where it executes changed.

float step(float tau_ext) {
  // semi-implicit Euler: update the speed first, then the position
  float acc = (tau_ext - Bd*x2 - Kd*x1) / Md;
  x2 += acc * Ts;   x1 += x2 * Ts;

  // rate limit
  float dref = (x1 - prev) / Ts;
  if (dref >  rate_max) dref =  rate_max;
  if (dref < -rate_max) dref = -rate_max;
  float ref = prev + dref * Ts;

  // position clamp
  if (ref >  theta_lim) ref =  theta_lim;
  if (ref < -theta_lim) ref = -theta_lim;

  x1 = ref;   // <-- THIS is the anti-windup
  prev = ref;
  return ref;
}

What each part does

  1. The first two lines move the pretend object one tick forward. The push (tau_ext), minus the pretend damper's resistance, minus the pretend spring's pull, divided by the pretend weight, gives how fast its speed is changing (acc). Add that to the speed (x2), then add the speed to the position (x1). Doing the speed first, then the position, is a little more stable than the other order.
  2. Rate limit: never ask the motor to move faster than rate_max, whatever the pretend object does. A safety cap on speed.
  3. Position clamp: never ask for an angle beyond theta_lim (±45°). The software version of the mechanical stop.
  4. Anti-windup, the single most important line: overwrite the pretend object's position with the clamped value. Without it, the pretend object keeps drifting past the limit while the real device sits against its stop; when the push reverses, the device does nothing for a long moment while the hidden gap unwinds. With it, the pretend model can never disagree with reality. It is one line, and it is the difference between a device that feels responsive and one that feels broken.

Put the 8 to 10 Hz low-pass filter on tau_ext before it enters step() (section 2.6).

In plain words

The controller does not want a cleaned-up EMG waveform. It wants one number, every 20 milliseconds, saying how hard the patient is trying, in torque units, plus two pieces of "how much should I trust this". The ML developer's job is to turn muscle signals into that packet. The controller's job is to use the packet safely, whatever is inside it.

The classical baseline (emg_baseline.m) does exactly this; a model that outputs the torque directly must reproduce the same outcome.
  • Rate: one packet every 20 ms. The classifier's feature window (200 ms with a 50 ms step) is a different consumer with different needs; do not reuse it for this output.
  • Training target: do not train the ML to copy the baseline's output and then claim it "beats" the baseline; the comparison becomes circular (D-21). Early smoke tests against the baseline or synthetic data are fine. The honest target is real measured torque, which needs the EMG-to-log sync link (section 5.1) to be restored.
  • Safety: because the hint is feedforward and outside the stability loop, a wrong hint degrades the assist but cannot destabilise it. That is not permission to be careless: a wild hint still moves a person's arm, up to the limits below.

6.5 The safety stack in one place

These are the limits that stand between a software mistake and a person's wrist, in the order a command meets them:

  1. The ML hint is bounded to the intent limit and can be ignored when valid is "no" (6.4).
  2. The 8 to 10 Hz low-pass smooths sudden jumps (2.6).
  3. The pretend model with Md ≥ 3Jh keeps the loop stable (2.2).
  4. The rate limit caps how fast the target can move (6.3).
  5. The position clamp stops the target at ±45° (6.3).
  6. Anti-windup keeps the model honest after any clamp (6.3).
  7. The sanity gates stop a trial if the torque estimate exceeds 0.98 N·m or the servo passes 60 °C (5.4).
  8. The hardware E-stop, checked every tick, overrides everything.

Where we stand, honestly

Read this first

7.1 Where you actually stand

Stronger than it feels. The control design is finished and defensible; the identification scripts are written and checked against synthetic truth; the decision and failure logs exist; and the architecture question that could have sunk the review has a one-sentence answer from a datasheet. What is missing is hardware in hand and numbers on paper. That is a procurement and assembly problem, not an engineering one.

You are also carrying more in your head than you need to. That is what this document is for. Nobody expects you to recite Part 2; they expect you to know where the answer lives and why it is the answer.

Real strengths

  • The measurement goal can be proved wrong by data, needs no patients, and does not depend on the ML working.
  • Testing on the surrogate first gives error bars on your own method, which almost no undergraduate project has.
  • The choice of admittance control was forced by the hardware, so it cannot be argued with.
  • Free torque sensing through the transmission: a real design insight, properly cited. Free on RUD (the cable), deliberate on FE (the added spring, D-23).
  • One decision (one axis at a time) solves two independent constraints.
  • The decision log and failure log make the work continuable by the next team and honest to a reader.
  • Two field interviews with a practising physiotherapist: primary evidence that changed the design in seven places rather than merely confirming it.
  • A specification traced from clinical practice (MDC ≤ 2°), not from an encoder datasheet.
  • Two protocol defects were caught before any subject was tested (F-06 and F-07), one of which would have produced a systematic bias indistinguishable from the effect being measured.

Real weaknesses: say them before a reviewer does

  • The number of subjects will be small and mostly healthy. Patient data may be zero.
  • Two EMG channels limit what can be claimed about classification.
  • The autoencoder idea is no longer novel: Anastasiev 2025, Mohammadiazni 2026 and Wang 2026 got there first (F-05).
  • Torque is estimated, not measured with a traceable sensor. Quantified, but indirect.
  • The 12-bit servo encoder is a resolution floor that cannot be improved.
  • Dyneema creep causes session-to-session drift, managed by protocol rather than removed.
  • No clinical validation. None is possible in nine weeks, and claiming otherwise is the fastest way to lose the viva.
  • MyoPro exists. It is a commercially sold, FDA-listed EMG-driven elbow-wrist-hand orthosis. EMG-driven upper-limb orthotics is not novel and we do not claim it (D-20).
  • The physiotherapist's own view: "EMG is not very necessary for wrist evaluations." That is correct for assessment, which is why EMG earns its place only in the assist path, and why the ML is a challenger and not a foundation (D-21).
  • ±45° hard stops cannot deliver terminal-range stretching (normal range is about 80° flexion and 70° extension). Adjustable stops were requested; if refused, it is a stated scope limit.

What could actually kill it

  • Mechanical assembly slipping. Risk number one by a wide margin. Everything downstream waits on it.
  • Procurement. Standard post to Sri Lanka is 3 to 6 weeks. Buy locally at 2 to 3 times the price and treat the markup as insurance.
  • Test 6 failing. A shell under 36 N·m/rad invalidates every measurement. Find out early, not in November.
  • Team coupling. If the ML side does not build to the contract in 6.4, integration becomes a negotiation instead of a wiring job.

7.2 What to claim, and what never to

Claim this

A forearm-grounded, two-axis wrist platform that measures joint mechanical impedance with stated uncertainty, checked against calibrated mechanical standards, and that renders a commanded target feel with measured stability margins across a defined range of impairment.

Never claim this

Clinical effectiveness. Improved recovery. Readiness for patient use. Novelty of the autoencoder approach. Any number you have not personally measured on this hardware. Each of these is a question you cannot answer, handed to a panel for free.

7.3 The fallback ladder: decide the trigger now, not in November

The most useful thing for your own peace of mind is to decide in advance what you will do if the mechanism is late. Write it into the plan so the decision is already made when you are tired and short of time.

PLAN A

Mechanism working by 2 October

The full programme: a measurement study on 8 to 10 healthy subjects on both axes, the assist mode shown with measured stability margins, ML integration through the replay harness. This is the thesis you designed.

PLAN B

Mechanism slips to mid-October

A full measurement study on the surrogate, plus a single axis on human subjects. The assist mode shown qualitatively rather than quantified across the range. Still a complete, honest thesis: the measurement chapter carries it.

PLAN C

Mechanism does not arrive

One servo, one encoder, the surrogate rig: a single-axis bench instrument. Complete validation of the identification method against calibrated standards. This is still a thesis, and still publishable as a methods paper. The contribution is the measurement technique and its uncertainty, which was always the strongest part.

The trigger: if the mechanism is not producing motion under servo command by 16 October, drop to Plan B without debating it. That date leaves four weeks for data collection and four for writing, the minimum either task survives on.
